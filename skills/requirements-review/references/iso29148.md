## Clause 5.2.5: the nine characteristics of an individual requirement
Necessary (needed, still applicable, not obsolete; a requirement with an expiry date must say so), Appropriate (level of detail and abstraction fits the level of the entity; avoid unnecessary architecture/design constraints), Unambiguous (can be understood only one way, stated simply), Complete (enough to be understood without other information), Singular (one capability/characteristic/constraint/quality factor; several conditions are permitted), Feasible (achievable within constraints with acceptable risk), Verifiable (worded so it can be proved; measurable is easier to verify), Correct (represents the original need correctly), Conforming (follows the approved template and style, when there is one).

BABOK has these that 29148 does not name: Consistent (in 29148 it is a characteristic of the **whole set**), Concise, Prioritized, Understandable.

## Clause 5.2.6: the five characteristics of the whole set of requirements
Complete (no TBD, TBS or TBR left; they may be temporary during development, but the set is not considered complete until all are resolved; the acceptable time frame depends on risk and dependencies), Consistent (each requirement unique, no conflict or overlap; units and measurement systems uniform; one term one meaning), Feasible (the whole set can be done within constraints; includes the idea of "affordable"), Comprehensible (clear what is expected and the relation to the system that contains it), Able to be validated (meeting the set of requirements achieves the need within constraints of cost, schedule, technology, law and regulation).
How to use: at the whole-set review step, also check leftover TBD/TBS/TBR, mixed units of measure, two requirements that overlap.

## Clauses 5.2.4 and 5.2.7: writing and wording
- A good requirement contains at least one of: something the system must meet to solve a problem/achieve a goal/handle a concern; a measurable condition; being limited by a constraint; describing a capability of the **system** rather than of the user or operator; being verifiable.
- A natural-language sentence needs a subject (system/software) and an action or constraint. Condition-action tables and use cases are also ways to record requirements.
- The keywords should be **agreed beforehand** in the project. The common practice the standard names: **shall** = binding; **will** = a fact, future, statement of purpose, or context/limits of use (not binding); **should** = preference or goal, **not a requirement**; **may** = suggestion or permission; descriptive sentences use "is/are". Avoid **must** (easily taken as a requirement), avoid negative statements ("shall not"), avoid the passive voice, avoid "shall be able to". In Vietnamese: map manually, for example "phải" = shall, "nên" = should, "có thể" = may, and state this convention in the document; apply it only when the organization/contract chooses that convention.
- Note in the standard: in agile, other expressions such as user stories can be used without "shall" (NOTE 2 in 5.2.4).
- Terms specific to requirements engineering must be formally defined and used consistently; definitions are written as statements, not as requirements.
- The word groups to avoid (5.2.7), used as a screening list for Unambiguous and Verifiable: superlatives ("best"); subjective language ("user-friendly", "easy to use", "cost effective"); vague pronouns ("it", "this"); adverbs/adjectives with no measure ("almost always", "significant", "minimal") and vague logic ("or", "and/or"; when you meet "and/or" consider splitting into several requirements); open-ended phrases that cannot be verified ("support", "but not limited to", "at least"); comparatives ("better"); loopholes ("if possible", "as appropriate"); universal quantifiers ("all", "always", "never", "every"; hard to verify); insufficient references (missing date, version, applicable section).
- A requirement states "what", not "how"; but when decomposing to lower levels it may record design decisions from the higher level (5.2.7).
- Every assumption related to a requirement must be recorded and confirmed in its attributes (for example rationale) or in an accompanying document (5.2.7).

## Clause 5.2.8: requirement attributes
The standard gives **examples** (not a mandatory list): Identification (unique; **never changed, never reused** even when the requirement is deleted), Version, Owner (the person/unit that maintains it, approves changes, reports status), Stakeholder Priority (stakeholder consensus; scale 1 to 5 or High/Medium/Low; priority does not mean other requirements are unnecessary but shows candidates for trade-off), Risk (including feasibility/technology maturity, can be inherited from the parent requirement), Rationale (the reason, pointing to supporting analysis), Difficulty (for example Easy/Normal/Hard), Type.
Example Types: Functional/Performance (performance is an attribute of a function; **a performance requirement standing alone is not enough**, usually expressed quantitatively), Interface, Process (process requirements from contract/organization, including legal compliance), Quality/Non-functional, Usability/Quality-in-use, Human factors. The standard points to ISO/IEC 25030 and 25010 for software quality requirements.
Note: **Status, Source, Traceability up/down are not in the example list in 5.2.8**; they are still useful attributes (see BABOK 3.4 and `requirements-trace`) but do not attribute them to 29148.

## Clause 6.5.2.2: verification
It recommends attaching the verification method when the requirement is created; record it in the verification/traceability matrix or the verification plan. The verification method answers: how (with success criteria), who, when (preferably event-driven, not by calendar date), where. **The four standard methods**: inspection (examination, comparing against documents), analysis (analysis, modelling, simulation; can be based on "similarity" under strict conditions), demonstration (qualitative demonstration, few instruments), test (quantitative testing under controlled conditions). Use a "Verification method" column in the traceability matrix/test set with these four values when the organization follows a systems-engineering approach.

## Clause 9: content of information items
- Clause 9.2 (general): identification (title, revision notice), front matter (table of contents, list of figures, tables), definitions, references (preferably separate "conformance" from "guidance"), abbreviations.
- **The standard prescribes content, not the order or structure of sections**: "the order and structure of sections may be chosen according to the project's information management policy" (repeated at 9.3.1, 9.4.1, 9.6.1). So there is no mandatory "standard section frame" to catch order errors; only check whether the content is complete.
- The content lists of the BRS (9.3) and SRS (9.6) and the comparison with the skill's frame: see `document-skill` (`references/iso29148-content.md`).

## Where the compilation file was wrong (checked against the standard)
- It lists **10** characteristics per sentence, including **Consistent**: the standard has **9**, Consistent only at the whole-set level.
- It labels the set of 5 characteristics as "Affordable/Feasible" and "Comprehensible/Bounded": the standard says Feasible (with a note "includes the idea of affordable") and Comprehensible; there is no "Bounded".
- It says SHOULD is "recommended, not mandatory in the first release" and WILL is "third-party responsibility": the standard says should is a preference/goal, **not a requirement**, and will is a fact/future/purpose or context.
- It attributes a list of 12 attributes (Status, Source, Verification Method, Traceability up/down, MoSCoW) to "Clause 5.3": 5.3 is *Practical considerations* (iteration and recursion); the attributes are in 5.2.8 and are a different 8 examples; the priority scale in the standard is 1 to 5 or High/Medium/Low, with no mention of MoSCoW.
- BRS: the file lists 13 items and assigns self-invented sub-headings; the standard's 9.3 has 18 content items (9.3.2 to 9.3.19, including business quality, structure, high-level operational concept and scenarios, life-cycle concepts, project constraints).
- SRS: the file uses an IEEE 830-style structure and attributes "ISO 25010" to section 3.7; the standard's 9.6 has 19 content items (9.6.2 to 9.6.20) with different names and order; the quality attributes part 9.6.18 only names Reliability, Availability, Security, Maintainability, Portability as examples.
- It says the standard replaces IEEE 830, 1233, 1362 and ISO/IEC TR 19759: the standard only states explicitly that it **cancels and replaces the 2011 edition**; nothing else is seen in the parts read.
