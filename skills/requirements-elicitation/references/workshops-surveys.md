# Workshops, surveys, collaborative games, glossary, interfaces, communication (BABOK; paraphrased)

## Workshop (10.50, pp. 363–366)
Has: a group of representative stakeholders, a defined objective, interactive work, a **defined output**, a facilitator.
- **Preparation**: purpose and desired outcome; participants; facilitator and scribe; agenda; how outputs are recorded; schedule and invitations; room and equipment; send materials beforehand; interview before the workshop if needed.
- **Roles**: Sponsor (ultimately responsible, usually does not attend), Facilitator (keeps a professional tone, stays on objective, applies ground rules, guides decisions and resolves conflict, lets everyone speak), Scribe (records decisions in a predefined format, tracks deferred items), Timekeeper (optional), Participants.
- **Running it**: open with the purpose and outcome; a light warm-up is possible; agree ground rules (respect opinions, everyone contributes, tangents only in a separate time slot, discuss issues not people, how decisions are made); the facilitator keeps checking activities against the purpose.
- **After**: handle open items, finalize documents, send to attendees and those who need to know.
- A BA acting as both expert and facilitator easily confuses the roles: be careful. Limits: hard to schedule, depends on facilitator skill; too many people is slow, too few misses needs.
- Session output template: decisions, open items (Item Tracking), actions, owner, deadline.

## Survey/questionnaire (10.45, pp. 350–352)
- Preparation: clear objective; target group (size, cultural, language and location differences); question types; sample (a statistically representative sample is needed if you want to generalize); distribution and collection channel; **acceptable response rate and closing deadline** (below the threshold, usefulness is limited); whether to add interviews before/after; write questions that stay on the objective; **pilot** to catch errors.
- Closed questions (easy to analyze, used when the range of answers is known) and open questions (detailed, used when the range of answers is unknown, costly to classify). Use neutral language and do not arrange questions to steer respondents toward the "desired" answer.
- When distributing: state the objective, how the results will be used, and the anonymity/confidentiality commitment; choose the channel by urgency, security level and geographic spread.
- When recording results: consolidate, summarize, look for emerging themes.

## Collaborative games (10.10, p. 243 onward)
Structured play with rules, aimed at sharing understanding, exposing tacit assumptions and building a shared model; often has a visual and tactile element (sticky notes, drawing). A neutral facilitator is advisable. Only the opening section was read; the catalogue of specific games has not been read.

## Glossary (10.23, pp. 285–287)
Add a term when: it is domain-specific, has several definitions, has a definition that differs from ordinary meaning, or is easy to misunderstand. Create it early; have a person responsible; keep definitions short; spell out abbreviations; make it easy for everyone to access, with only a few people allowed to edit. It needs an owner, otherwise it goes stale. In this skill: each elicitation session should produce a list of new/differently-used terms.

## Interface analysis (10.24, p. 287 onward)
Helps identify who, what information (with volume), when/how often, where, why and how between components/systems. Interface types: user, external party (regulator), process, data between systems, API, device. Identifying them early shows which stakeholders must be invited. ## Confirming and communicating (4.3, 4.4, 4.5; pp. 66–72)
- Confirmation checks results against the source and against other results; document analysis, interviews, reviews and workshops are often used; domain SMEs help find gaps, inconsistencies and conflicts.
- Communication is two-way and iterative: identify the recipients, content, purpose, context and expected outcome. Do not simply send and assume it was understood; change the approach if they are not getting it. Ask: who is the audience, what do they need, what style do they prefer, are there regulatory/contractual constraints. Forms: formal documents, informal, presentations. Platforms: group (instant discussion), individual, email/non-verbal (once the information is mature). Communication is done when the recipient **understands** the content and its consequences.
- Managing collaboration: poor relationships lead to poor information, negative reactions, resistance and ignored information. Stakeholders' roles, influence and attitudes change over time; monitor and update.

## Agile techniques that support elicitation (Agile Extension v2; paraphrased)
- **Impact mapping (7.3, pp. 62–63)**: a four-level map of Goal (why), Actor (who can influence the goal), Impact (what they do differently), Deliverable (which functions help them). Done face to face, facilitated, working from the goal downwards; keep it visible so it can be edited. Good for preventing scope creep and for starting from value instead of features.
- **Persona (7.7, pp. 71–73)**: a representative character with a name, traits, motivations, needs and differentiators; there is a short (one-page) and a long form. Use when the team cannot reach real customers; user research is needed to flesh it out. A persona is an assumption unless it rests on real data: label it [Assumption].
- **Story mapping (7.20)**: a group session to build the chain of activities, find missing stories and choose which stories to elaborate. Prepare the themes and stories before the session.
- **Kano (7.5)** when you need to ask customers about features (questions about having/not having the feature); the "threshold" characteristics are often forgotten because everyone assumes they exist, so they must be asked about directly.
